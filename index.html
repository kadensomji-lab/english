Climb to Safety

Fifty years ago, a storm that refused to move turned a Colorado canyon into a raging river in less than four hours. What separated the people who survived from the ones who did not came down to one simple choice.

Part I

The Radio Call

Transmission · 9:15 PM
UNIT: Sgt. W.H. Purdy | LOC: U.S. Hwy 34, 0.5 mi E of Drake
"I'm stuck. I'm right in the middle of it. I can't get out."

"I'm stuck. I'm right in the middle of it. I can't get out."

Those were the last words anyone heard from Sgt. Willis Hugh Purdy. It was around 9:15 p.m. on July 31, 1976. He was on U.S. Highway 34, half a mile east of Drake, Colorado, and the Big Thompson River was rising fast around his patrol car. He gave dispatch his location, and then he asked for one more thing. He told them to get the cars out of the low area below him.

Purdy had already finished his shift for the day. When dispatchers told him the weather in the canyon was turning dangerous, he drove in anyway and spent his last hours warning dozens of people to get out. His order to clear the lower canyon is credited with saving hundreds of lives. He was never heard from again.

He was one of 144 people who died that night in what is still Colorado's deadliest natural disaster. Five people were never found. Researchers later found that surviving came down to one basic move. People had to get out of the water's path and go uphill. Lori Peek, director of the Natural Hazards Center at the University of Colorado Boulder, says the people in the highest danger were those who stayed in their vehicles. This story is about that choice, and why it was so hard to make.

Part II

The Canyon

July 31 was the night before Colorado's 100th birthday, and thousands of people had come to Big Thompson Canyon for the centennial weekend. Estimates of how many were inside that night range from 2,500 to 3,500. The canyon runs about 25 miles from Estes Park to Loveland between steep rock walls, with Highway 34 beside the river. According to Denver Gazette archives, the river was usually only about 18 inches deep.

Above them, a massive storm stalled against the mountains. NOAA scientists later found that strong easterly winds pushed warm, moist air up the slope, and the storm stopped moving. Up to 7.5 inches of rain fell in about an hour, and about 12 inches fell in a few hours, according to the U.S. Geological Survey. The steep slopes could not hold it. "It was almost like a bathtub effect," said Neil Grigg, an engineering professor at Colorado State University.

Part III

The Night

Timeline Along the 25-Mile Canyon

5:00 PM

Rain Starts in the Canyon

Rain begins falling around 5 p.m. while thousands of holiday visitors are camped and driving along Highway 34.

The rain started at 5 p.m. and was heavy by 6:30. Around 8, people began calling 911 about rocks on the highway. By 8:45, a patrolman near Estes Park reported flooding, and deputies began driving through campgrounds with loudspeakers, circling two or three times. Some people left right away. Others waited until the second or third pass. At least two never left their campsites, and they died.

Most people were never warned at all. In Drake, where the flood hit hardest, the only warnings were what people could see and hear: the heavy rain, the rising river, and a sudden change in the sound of the water. One couple in Waltonia knew the river so well that when its sound changed, they moved right away. They already had a plan in case the dam at Estes Park failed. Another couple was stopped by a patrolman who told them to leave their car and climb. They thought he was overreacting, so they stayed. They died, according to Eve Gruntfest, a University of Colorado researcher who studied the flood in 1977.

At 9:15, Purdy made his last radio call. Around 10:30, a wall of water nearly 20 feet tall tore down the canyon. The National Weather Service issued its first flash-flood bulletin at 11 p.m., about thirty minutes after the main wave had already hit.

Part IV

The Ledge

Connie Granath Hays was 18. She and her friend Terry Belair were driving home from Estes Park when they entered the canyon in a heavy downpour. As the water rose, they left her car and climbed the rock wall beside the road. The first ledge was not high enough. From there, they watched her car float away in the current, so they climbed higher.

They held on until daybreak, when a helicopter finally lifted them out. Fifty years later, Granath Hays told Denver7 that the two of them realized "how very lucky we were." Their choice was the simplest one available: leave the car and go up.

Part V

The Morning After

When the light came, helicopters lifted more than 800 people off ledges and rooftops. In Loveland, refrigerated trucks were parked outside the hospital morgue. In the end, 144 people were dead, and the flood had destroyed 418 homes and 52 businesses. Estes Park Officer Michael Conley also died helping victims, along with Purdy. Five people were never found: Teresa Graham, 10, Evelyn Kindred, 67, George McCarty, 21, Vernon Oler, 51, and Rochelle Rogers, 2.

Gruntfest's research helped lead to the yellow warning signs now posted in canyons across Colorado: "In case of flash flood, climb to safety." Peek says the sign has lasted because "the message has to be actionable."

The sign does not ask anyone to read a rain gauge or judge the sky. It asks them to go up. Almost everything about that night was complicated. The one thing that was not was where to go.

About This Story

I picked a flash flood because I kept thinking about how quickly water can change a place that looks completely safe. What stood out to me most was how simple the survival advice is, and how many things get in the way of following it. I used John Branch's "Snow Fall" as my mentor text. I did not interview anyone for this story, so every fact and quote comes from the published sources below.
